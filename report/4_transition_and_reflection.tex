\section{Transition and Reflection}

\subsection{Decomposition strategy}
The transition into a distributed architecture using microservices was an incremental approach
where we tackled one problem at a time, made a change, rolled out the change, assessed it, and continued to the next problem.
This approach follows the suggestion by PERSON in his book \_\_\_\_ where he also cites Martin Fowler:
\emph{''If you do a big-bang rewrite, the only thing you’re guaranteed of is a big
bang''}. SIDE 102 microservice bog.

Our choices of decomposing the monolith into microservices focused on modelling around the business domain.
That is, creating microservices around meaningful parts of the product and different business capabilities rather than technical layers.
This creates clear service boundaries with components conforming to well-defined interfaces and 
aligns the architecture with the organisation, encouraging empowered teams that can focus on delivering value within their specific domain.

\subsection{Key design decisions in the transition}

The first major design decision was figuring out how to move our user authentication and session management from a monolithic to a distributed system.
Our primary goal was to ensure the frontend could be horizontally scaled with ease as we anticipated it would handle the
most significant load. This meant going away from the inherent statefulness of the monoliths \verb|current_user| context.
%  towards making a stateless frontend.

One approach was to have each frontend instance store session data in its own memory. This would require maintaining of state
or use of sticky sessions in the load balancer, which would cause significant limitations to scalability and reduce resilience.
% Måske kan vi bare skrive "to avoid these isues we made the frotnend sateless"
To avoid these issues we made the frontend stateless, by being stored in the browser cookie, allowing it to scale easily on demand with multiple interchangable instances.

Next we considered how to handle profile data. A solution would be to store this alongside the credentials in the authentication service.
However, this would contradict the concept of modelling microservices around the business domain and seperating concerns since profile information and 
authentication relates to two different concepts. One being security, another being general personal information.
This also becomes clear due to the fact that a Provider does not have the need for a personal profile, but still needs to authenticate.
Separating handling of authentication and profile into two dedicated microservices also allows us to scale the two services independently.

Another challenge we encountered was how to always provide information about the user's authentication to our HTML templates, as the nav bar adapts based on this.
Since this information is always needed we didn't want to pass it every time we rendered a template and instead searched for a similar
solution to the \texttt{current\_user} object always accessible from templates in the monolith.
Our chosen design utilizes the decorator \texttt{@app.before\_request} to check if the user is authenticated with the Auth service on every request. 
We use this to populate the \texttt{g.user} object with whether the user is authenticated and their role. By making this available as a variable named 
\texttt{current\_user} through a context processor we were able to directly reuse the logic of our old templates.
As we always call the authentication service before requests we also simplified the logic in our routes and \texttt{@login\_required} and \texttt{@role\_required} decorators.
One downside to the chosen approach is that this function is called on every single request which includes static requests. 
However, by instantly returning from the function in case of static requests this overhead becomes negligible. 
In the future the design might cause unnecessary overhead if our frontend implements multiple routes that don't render the nav bar.

An additional issue to address was handling images, which can be broken into two subproblems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Uploading and storing images
    \item Downloading images
\end{itemize}
For the first problem of storing images, an approach would be to have a dedicated database for images that each of the microservices interacted with,

Our system implements lazy profile creation, meaning profiles are
created on-demand when seekers first visit their profile page through
a PUT request which creates or replaces the profile.
This design choice prioritizes simplicity over immediate consistency
as seekers exist without profiles until they choose to create one.

We also considered eager profile creation, where a seeker's profile would be
created during registration. This adds a level of consistency but requires distributed transaction to maintain consistency between the separate Auth and Profile databases.

One way to achieve this is through the use of a two-phase commit (2PC)
to ensure that both services either commit or abort the transaction.
This would prioritize consistency as all nodes commit or abort together,
but sacrifices availability since participants must all be available and 
wait until a decision is made.

Another approach is to use the Saga pattern.
That is, instead of using a distributed transaction, we maintain data consistency
through a sequence of local transactions.
This could be implemented with a centralized Saga orchestrator.
Firstly a user in the Auth service would be created. On success, the profile in the Profiles service is created. If this fails, we would either compensate 
by deleting the user from Auth or repeat the request if we see user creation as the pivot.

In our case lazy profile creation is the right choice as for profile creation the added complexity is not warranted. 



\subsection{Future improvement}
One area that can be improved upon is our handling of data consistency
when deleting collectives from the system.
This operation requires coordinating two services (Collectives and Pictures)
to delete related data, which illustrates the challenge of maintaining consistency
across microservices.

Our current implementation uses a simple sequential approach:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Delete the image from Pictures service
    \item Delete the collective from Collectives service
\end{enumerate}

We only go to step 2 if step 1 succeeds, however, we do not
ensure the success of step 2.
Because of this, we deliberately chose to delete images first to prevent orphaned images
in our system.
With this ordering if we fail in step two, the user can at least try to delete the collective again.
However, the data in our system can be left in an inconsistent state which is not optimal.
% Man kunne også bare nævne at en løsning ville være at anvende en Saga appraoch her og så 
% eventuelt slette hele "Profile creation" afsnittet.
Furthermore, our current approach exhibits high temporal coupling as both services must be available simultaneously for the operation to succeed.

One way to improve both the resilience and reduce the coupling would be to use an event-based approach.
Here we could publish an event from the collectives service on deletion and have the Pictures service subscribe to the event. 
This approach keeps the services referentially decoupled. If we allow the Pictures service to receive notifications form the past they will also be temporally decoupled, 
as the Pictures service can cleanup later, even if it is temporarily unavailable at the time of deletion.
It does, however, introduce some complexity but ensures eventual consistency and reduces coupling which is a worthwhile trade-off.



\subsection{Conclusion}
All of these design decisions led us to the chosen autonomous microservices. The design promotes openness, scalability and a high degree of distribution transparency 
by making the microservices work together without users or applications having to know all the details.
We strived to only have domain coupling which is a loose form of coupling in our system. 
The frontend microservice has high centralisation of logic
having to interact with several downstream microservices, and as such it would have to be replicated and scaled accordingly.
The next steps of our product development would be the implementation of applications, and in turn
the payment processing. Here we presume stricter consistency requirements will be encountered where the Saga pattern would be justified. 
An early improvement to our architecture by the introduction of an event bus might also support this further development.
